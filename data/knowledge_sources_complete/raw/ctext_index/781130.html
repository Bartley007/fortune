''Li Xu Zhong's Book of Destiny'' ('Li Xu Zhong Ming Shu')

'Li Xu Zhong's Book of Destin'y, a three-volume work, is traditionally attributed to Ghost Valley Master (鬼谷子) and annotated by Li Xu Zhong during the Tang Dynasty. Li Xu Zhong, whose courtesy name was Changrong, was the eighth-generation descendant of Li Chong, a former Imperial Scholar (进士) who served as Imperial Censor (殿中侍御史) during the Yuanhe era. The renowned scholar Han Yu wrote a tombstone inscription for Li Xu Zhong, which is included in the 'Changli Collection of Essays' (昌黎文集). Li Xu Zhong is revered as the progenitor of the practice of fortune-telling through astrology in subsequent generations.

Han Yu's inscription mentions that Li Xu Zhong was deeply versed in the 'Five Elements' (五行书) and used the day, month, and year of a person's birth, along with the corresponding zodiac and heavenly stems and earthly branches, to predict their lifespan, social status, and fortune with remarkable accuracy. However, Han Yu did not indicate that Li Xu Zhong had written a book. The 'Tang Dynasty Catalogue of Arts and Literature' (唐书艺文志) does not list this work, but it appears in the 'Song Dynasty Catalogue' (宋志) as 'Li Xu Zhong's Destiny Book and Patterns' in two volumes. Zheng Qiao's 'Catalogue of Arts and Literature' (艺文略) lists it as 'Li Xu Zhong's Astrology' in one volume and 'Supplement to the Destiny Book' in another. Chao Gongwu's 'Reading Catalogue' (读书志) records it as 'Li Xu Zhong's Book of Fate' in three volumes. Jiao Hong's 'Catalogue of Classics and Books' (经籍志) adds another volume, 'Supplement to the Destiny Book', to the three-volume edition. The discrepancies in the titles and volumes of the book make it challenging to verify its authenticity.

The book has been out of circulation for a long time, making it difficult to reconcile these differences. However, the 'Yongle Encyclopedia' (永乐大典) contains a relatively complete version of the text, with a coherent sequence of chapters. It includes a preface written by Li Xu Zhong, in which he claims that the philosopher Sima Jizhu encountered Ghost Valley Master on the southern slope of Mount Hu and received nine chapters of 'Esoteric Texts' (逸文) that discuss profound truths. Li Xu Zhong states that he compiled and annotated these texts, drawing on various sources.

The content of the book primarily focuses on the sixty cyclical years (甲子), aligning with the method described in Han Yu's inscription, which emphasizes the year, month, and day of birth. However, the latter part of the book frequently mentions the four pillars (四柱), a concept that emerged during the Song Dynasty, which contradicts the earlier sections. Additionally, many of the official titles and references are characteristic of the Song Dynasty, suggesting that the book was not entirely written by Li Xu Zhong. Some parts are ancient and difficult to understand, likely written by Tang Dynasty scholars, while others are crude and likely added later.

Given the mixed nature of the text, it is suspected that the original Tang Dynasty version of the book was later augmented by Song Dynasty astrologers, who attributed their own theories to Li Xu Zhong's annotations of Ghost Valley Master's work to lend credibility to their methods. Despite these issues, the book offers valuable insights into the principles of astrology, which are more logical and precise compared to later, more mystical interpretations. Therefore, the work has been preserved in three volumes, as per Chao Gongwu's original catalog, with annotations to clarify the parts that are clearly later additions, to prevent readers from being misled.
